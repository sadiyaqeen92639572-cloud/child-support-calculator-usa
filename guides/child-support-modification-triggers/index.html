What Triggers a Child Support Modification?

Life changes, but a child support order doesn't update itself. To change an existing order, most states require you to show that circumstances have changed enough to meet a specific legal standard — and many set an actual numeric threshold for what "enough" means.

Common qualifying changes

The most common triggers are a significant, sustained change in either parent's income (a job loss, a big raise, a new job), a change in the custody or parenting-time schedule, a change in the child's needs (a new medical condition, for example), or simply enough time passing since the last order — many states allow a no-fault review every few years regardless of whether anything else changed.

Why a small change usually isn't enough

Courts generally won't reopen a case over a minor fluctuation — that would make every order permanently unstable. Most states set a specific percentage (commonly in the 10-20% range) or dollar-amount difference between the old order and what current guidelines would produce, below which a court presumes the change isn't "substantial" enough to modify.

Checking your state's exact threshold

The exact percentage, dollar floor, and any waiting period since the last order varies significantly by state — some states use a pure percentage test, others combine a percentage with a minimum dollar change, and a few use no fixed number at all, leaving it to a general case-by-case standard.

FAQ

Does meeting the threshold automatically change my order?

No — meeting a state's numeric threshold generally means you have grounds to file a modification petition, not that the amount changes automatically. You still need to file, and in most states, get a court's approval.
