What Does Child Support Actually Cover?

Child support generally isn't a reimbursement for specific receipts — it's calculated to cover a child's proportional share of the costs of running a household, and the receiving parent typically isn't required to itemize how it's spent.

The core categories

Guideline formulas are generally built around economic data on what households at a given income level typically spend raising children — covering housing, food, clothing, transportation, and other everyday costs, distributed across the household rather than tracked per child.

What's usually added on top

Health insurance premiums and work-related childcare are commonly added to the base calculation as separate line items rather than assumed to be included — see our guide on health insurance and childcare add-ons. Uninsured medical expenses above a certain amount are also frequently split separately.

What it typically doesn't require itemizing

Because the base amount is meant to cover ordinary costs broadly, the receiving parent generally isn't required to account for exactly how each dollar was spent — a common misconception is that support has to be spent on the child's direct costs only, dollar-for-dollar, which isn't how most guideline formulas are designed to work.

FAQ

Can I ask for an accounting of how child support is spent?

In most states, no — courts generally don't require itemized accounting of ordinary support payments, since the amount is meant to cover a child's broad share of household costs, not specific receipts.

Are extracurricular activities or private school included?

This varies by state and by case — some states treat these as deviations or additional add-ons that require a specific court finding, rather than assuming they're covered by the base amount.
