The Self-Support Reserve: How Low-Income Child Support Cases Work

Standard child support formulas can produce unrealistic results for very low-income parents — a straight percentage of a minimum-wage income can leave someone unable to cover their own basic living costs. Many states build in a specific protection against that.

What a self-support reserve does

A self-support reserve sets aside a baseline amount of a paying parent's income (often pegged to a multiple of the federal poverty guideline) before calculating support, or caps the support obligation so it doesn't push that parent's remaining income below the reserve amount.

How it typically changes the calculation

In states with a self-support reserve, low-income cases are often calculated differently than higher-income cases — sometimes using the paying parent's own income alone rather than combined income, sometimes applying a reduced percentage, sometimes flooring the result at a fixed minimum order rather than letting it hit zero.

It's not the same as a minimum order

Some states have both a self-support reserve and a separate flat minimum order (a smallest possible support amount, often in the $50-100/month range, that applies regardless of how low income is) — these are related but distinct mechanisms, and not every state has both.

FAQ

Does every state have a self-support reserve?

No — this protection varies significantly by state; some have a well-defined reserve amount tied to federal poverty guidelines, others don't formally use this concept at all.

Will my calculator show if this applies to me?

Yes — every state calculator on this site that has a self-support reserve mechanism will show a note in your results when it affects your calculation.
